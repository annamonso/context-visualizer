\chapter{Conclusions and Future Work}
\label{chap:conclusions}

This chapter closes the thesis: it checks the outcome against the
requirements of Chapter~\ref{chap:problem}, states the design lessons
the work argues for, and lays out the directions it opens.

\section{Conclusions}
\label{sec:conclusions}

This thesis set out from a one-sentence gap: the tools we surveyed answer
``what did my agent do?'' with a span tree of one run on one machine,
and, to our knowledge, none answers ``what is my agent \emph{fleet}
doing across the cluster, and which node is dragging it down?'' The claim defended across the preceding
chapters is that the gap closes naturally once the data is stored in its
native shape: a multi-agent execution is a causally-ordered event log, and
a distributed shared log with a capture process on every node (ChronoLog)
makes the coupling of agent semantics to physical topology a property
of the substrate rather than an integration problem.

Checking the outcome against the requirements of
\S\ref{sec:problem-requirements}, each now with its measured answer from
Chapter~\ref{chap:evaluation}: \textbf{R1} (dual-plane causal capture)
is met by the two capture paths and the \texttt{role@scenario} identity
contract, under which every event carries its host and the planes join by
construction, verified end-to-end by the click-through check of the
live harness. \textbf{R2} (real-time visibility) and \textbf{R3}
(durability with exactly-once ingest) are met jointly by the hot/cold
split: an event reaches the operator's screen 10.7\,ms after emission
and the durable archive 20--30\,s after (the hot path three orders
of magnitude inside the cold), with at-least-once spooled capture
deduplicated by high-water marks rebuilt from the log itself.
\textbf{R4} (fleet scale) required changing both computation and
representation: per-(host, minute) rollups hold the fleet read at
19--24\,ms across a $50\times$ growth in event volume where the raw
fold grows to $457\times$ slower, and the heatmap answers at 250 nodes
in 36\,ms where node-link rendering fails at a dozen.

\textbf{R5}
(minimal overhead) holds structurally and is confirmed empirically: the
agent-side mechanism costs 3.6\,ms per turn-plus-delegation, under
0.05\% of a mean turn, and the end-to-end A/B delta over real
LLM turns is statistically indistinguishable from zero. \textbf{R6}
(zero-cost diagnosis) rests on the deterministic
detect--cluster--diagnose pipeline with its self-compacting cross-run
memory, LLM assistance strictly opt-in; ground truth confirms the
pipeline correct: 580/580 planted faults detected with zero false
positives, a 500-signal storm compressed to one ranked card, and recurrence
recognized across runs. \textbf{R7} (drop-in deployment) is
demonstrated by the live harness, which attaches the full stack to a
plain SLURM allocation without modifying ChronoLog, the scheduler, or
the agent SDK: 28/28 automated checks on 4- and 8-node clusters,
sustained at 32 concurrent real agents with flat per-node sidecar
load.

Beyond the artifact, the thesis argues two transferable design lessons.
The first is architectural. Observability systems are conventionally
assembled as instrumentation plus a bespoke backend; for workloads
whose events are few, expensive, and causally structured (as
LLM-agent fleets are), inverting the architecture around a general
distributed shared log yields the cross-layer joins, the durability,
and the replayability for free, at the price of engineering around the
log's read behavior. That price is real but modest, and the evaluation
quantified both of its parts: the planned part (the hot/cold split of
Chapter~\ref{chap:design}) and the unplanned part
(\S\ref{sec:eval-limitations}'s broken replay path, absorbed by
generalizing archive-first reads at a bounded cost of half a minute of
staleness). What it buys is the capability neither surveyed camp
offers: the pivot from a fleet-wide symptom to a single agent's
conversation on a named machine. The case study measured that pivot at
72 seconds, questions to verified answers, for an operator who had
never seen the task before.

The second lesson comes from the same case study's baseline, and it
reframes what an observability \emph{interface} is for. The raw
captured log proved \emph{sufficient} for complete diagnosis (an
LLM agent given only the data directories answered all three
questions, orphaned delegation included, in under a minute), while
the human operator, given the same files, could not begin. The
dashboard's contribution is therefore not access to data but
\emph{encoded expertise}: each view is a frozen form of the queries an
expert would write. Systems built on a sufficient substrate can choose
where that expertise lives (in views for humans, or in agents that
query the log directly), and the shared-log architecture serves both
consumers from the same store.

\section{Future Work}
\label{sec:future}

\paragraph{Runtime causal queries.} The substrate stores the full causal
event history, but the analyses of Chapter~\ref{chap:analytics} are
pre-built views. The next step is the Pivot-Tracing
move~\cite{mace2015pivot}: ad-hoc queries evaluated along causal edges
(``the total token cost of everything that transitively touched agent
$B$''), which the log's ordering and the correlation schema already
make expressible.

\paragraph{Replicated hot path.} Promoting the hot store and live bus
from per-dashboard-process structures to a small replicated service
would remove the single point of live observation and allow multiple
dashboards over one fleet.

\paragraph{Recovering live replay.} The archive-first mode of
\S\ref{sec:eval-limitations} is the correct trade under the observed
substrate, but it leaves ChronoLog's player unused and pins
full-fidelity staleness to the drain window. Diagnosing the
transport-level fault beneath it (playback responses that never
reach one process's receiver while reaching another's on the same
host) is upstream work on the ChronoLog client/player pair; fixing
it would let the dashboard serve un-drained history live and shrink
the only staleness the design still carries.

\paragraph{Scale, sampling, and adaptivity.} Complete capture is the
right default at today's event rates; at orders of magnitude more
agents, selective capture of payload content (while always keeping the
causal skeleton) would be the principled degradation. Live validation at
hundreds of real nodes, closing the gap that
\S\ref{sec:eval-limitations} concedes, is chiefly an allocation problem.

\paragraph{Richer diagnosis.} The heuristic diagnoser's rule set could be
learned rather than hand-built (mining the incident memory across runs
for recurring signature--remediation pairs), and the opt-in LLM
diagnoser could be grounded on retrieved evidence (traces, fleet state)
rather than the incident summary alone.

\paragraph{Agent-facing observability.} The case-study baseline, an
LLM agent diagnosing the fleet from the raw log in under a minute,
is less a curiosity than a signpost: the next consumer of
observability data may itself be an agent. The captured log already
has everything such a consumer needs (causal order, correlation ids,
host attribution, JSONL it can read with four shell commands); the
obvious extension is a first-class query surface, an MCP tool over
the log and its analyses, so that a supervising agent can run the
Doctor's gather-and-detect loop, or answer ad-hoc causal questions,
as part of the fleet it watches. The zero-marginal-cost principle of
R6 would then bound how much of that supervision runs on heuristics
versus paid model calls.

\paragraph{Interoperability.} Emitting and ingesting the OpenTelemetry
generative-AI conventions~\cite{otelgenai} would let the system exchange
data with the Camp-1 ecosystem, their span semantics mapped onto the
log's event schema, so that adopting the fleet layer does not mean
abandoning existing per-agent tooling.

\medskip

The direction of the field is not in doubt: agent systems are growing in
both population and physical extent, and the questions operators ask are
increasingly the questions distributed systems have always asked, now
with a conversation attached. This thesis's contribution is to show that
the sixty-year-old toolbox of that discipline, from happened-before to
the shared log, is not merely applicable to the newest workload but is
its natural home.
