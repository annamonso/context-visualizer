\chapter{Conclusions and Future Work}
\label{chap:conclusions}

This chapter closes the thesis: it checks the outcome against the
requirements of Chapter~\ref{chap:problem}, states the design lessons
the work argues for, and lays out the directions it opens.

\section{Conclusions}
\label{sec:conclusions}

This thesis set out from a one-sentence gap: the tools we surveyed answer
``what did my agent do?'' with a span tree of one run on one machine,
and, to our knowledge, none answers ``what is my agent \emph{fleet}
doing across the cluster, and which node is dragging it down?'' The claim defended across the preceding
chapters is that the gap closes naturally once the data is stored in its
native shape: a multi-agent execution is a causally-ordered event log, and
a distributed shared log with a capture process on every node makes the
coupling of agent semantics to physical topology a property
of the substrate rather than an integration problem. That claim is about a
class of store, not a product. Section~\ref{sec:problem-substrate} makes the
distinction checkable by stating the contract a substrate must honour, and
Chapter~\ref{chap:design} is written against it, with
\S\ref{sec:design-chronolog} isolating what implementing it on ChronoLog
in particular required.

All seven requirements of \S\ref{sec:problem-requirements} are met, each
with a measured answer from Chapter~\ref{chap:evaluation}. \textbf{R1} is
met by the two capture paths and the \texttt{role@scenario} identity
contract, under which every event carries its host and the planes join by
construction. \textbf{R2} and \textbf{R3} are met jointly by the hot/cold
split: an event reaches the operator's screen 10.7\,ms after emission and
the durable archive 20--30\,s after, with at-least-once spooled capture
deduplicated by high-water marks rebuilt from the log itself. \textbf{R4}
required changing both computation and representation: per-(host, minute)
rollups hold the fleet read at 19--24\,ms across a $50\times$ growth in
event volume where the raw fold grows $457\times$ slower, and the heatmap
answers at 250 nodes in 36\,ms where node-link rendering fails at a dozen.
\textbf{R5} holds structurally and empirically: 3.6\,ms per
turn-plus-delegation, under 0.05\% of a mean turn, with an A/B delta
statistically indistinguishable from zero. \textbf{R6} rests on the
deterministic detect--cluster--diagnose pipeline, LLM assistance strictly
opt-in, scoring 580/580 planted faults with zero false positives and
compressing a 500-signal storm to one ranked card. \textbf{R7} is
demonstrated by the live harness: 28/28 automated checks on 4- and 8-node
clusters without modifying ChronoLog, the scheduler, or the agent SDK,
sustained at 32 concurrent real agents with flat per-node sidecar load.

Beyond the artifact, the thesis argues two transferable design lessons.
The first is architectural. Observability systems are conventionally
assembled as instrumentation plus a bespoke backend; for workloads
whose events are few, expensive, and causally structured (as
LLM-agent fleets are), inverting the architecture around a general
distributed shared log yields the cross-layer joins, the durability,
and the replayability for free, at the price of engineering around the
log's read behavior. That price is real but modest, and the evaluation
quantified both of its parts: the planned part (the hot/cold split of
Chapter~\ref{chap:design}) and the unplanned part
(\S\ref{sec:eval-limitations}'s broken replay path, absorbed by
generalizing archive-first reads at a bounded cost of half a minute of
staleness). What it buys is the capability neither surveyed camp
offers: the pivot from a fleet-wide symptom to a single agent's
conversation on a named machine. The case study measured that pivot at
72 seconds, questions to verified answers, for an operator who had
never seen the task before.

The second lesson comes from the same case study's baseline, and it
reframes what an observability \emph{interface} is for. The raw
captured log proved \emph{sufficient} for complete diagnosis (an
LLM agent given only the data directories answered all three
questions, orphaned delegation included, in under a minute), while
the human operator, given the same files, could not begin. The
dashboard's contribution is therefore not access to data but
\emph{encoded expertise}: each view is a frozen form of the queries an
expert would write. Systems built on a sufficient substrate can choose
where that expertise lives (in views for humans, or in agents that
query the log directly), and the shared-log architecture serves both
consumers from the same store.

\section{Future Work}
\label{sec:future}

\paragraph{From one substrate to many: a second implementation of the
contract.} The nearest-term direction, and the one already under way, is to
demonstrate the substrate independence the thesis argues for. The
\emph{specification} side of that work is done: \S\ref{sec:problem-substrate}
states which features a store must provide (S1--S5) and which it may omit
at a priced cost (S6--S9); \S\ref{sec:design-adapters} turns that contract
into the adapter interface, so that adapters declare \emph{capabilities}
(does this store support bounded-latency reads? enumeration? positional
resume?) rather than merely implement queries, and the hot/cold split
becomes a strategy chosen per substrate instead of a fixed architecture;
and \S\ref{sec:design-chronolog} predicts, feature by feature, what a port
would add and delete. What the contract has not yet had is a second
implementation to falsify it. The work is to write two or three (a
partitioned commit log such as Kafka; an embedded analytical store such as
DuckDB over the archive tiers; a plain local JSONL store for single-node
development, which the offline demo mode already approximates) and check the
predictions of Table~\ref{tab:substrate-systems} against what each port
actually costs. A prediction that survives makes the contract a
contribution; one that does not shows where the contract is
under-specified, which is the more useful outcome. Either way it converts
the artifact from a ChronoLog application into a portable
observability plug-in for multi-agent fleets, testable without a cluster and
adoptable by deployments that have no ChronoLog. In the same movement, emitting and ingesting the
OpenTelemetry generative-AI conventions~\cite{otelgenai} would make the
Camp-1 ecosystem one more supported backend, so that adopting the fleet
layer does not mean abandoning existing per-agent tooling.

\paragraph{Upstream convergence.} Two of the three constraints this design
works around are being removed at the source. In ongoing collaboration with
the ChronoLog developers (in particular Eneko Gonzalez), the blocking
GIL-holding replay of C2 and the missing catalog operation of C3 have since
been addressed upstream. Their removal would retire real machinery here: a
client that releases the interpreter lock while waiting makes the
pure-reader process split a preference rather than a necessity and permits
bounded-timeout live reads from the serving process, and a native
enumeration call makes the \texttt{\_\_index\_\_} chronicle redundant for
listing, leaving it useful only as a cheap first-seen ordering. C1, the
acceptance window, is inherent to the ingest-optimized design point and will
remain; the hot/cold split therefore stays, which is the correct outcome,
since it is the part of the read design that is about the class of substrate
rather than about one implementation of it. Re-measuring
\S\ref{sec:eval-freshness} against the fixed client, and re-deriving how much
of Chapter~\ref{chap:design} the fixes let us delete, is the natural
follow-up: the parts that survive a substrate fix are the parts that were
design rather than workaround. Recovering the live-replay path
(\S\ref{sec:eval-limitations}), whose transport-level cause remains
undiagnosed, belongs to the same upstream effort and would shrink the only
staleness the design still carries.

\paragraph{Runtime causal queries.} The substrate stores the full causal
event history, but the analyses of Chapter~\ref{chap:analytics} are
pre-built views. The next step is the Pivot-Tracing
move~\cite{mace2015pivot}: ad-hoc queries evaluated along causal edges
(``the total token cost of everything that transitively touched agent
$B$''), which the log's ordering and the correlation schema already
make expressible.

\paragraph{Replicated hot path, and scale.} Promoting the hot store and live
bus from per-dashboard-process structures to a small replicated service
would remove the single point of live observation and allow multiple
dashboards over one fleet. On the capture side, complete capture is the
right default at today's event rates; at orders of magnitude more agents,
selective capture of payload content (while always keeping the causal
skeleton) would be the principled degradation. Live validation at hundreds
of real nodes, closing the gap that \S\ref{sec:eval-limitations} concedes,
is chiefly an allocation problem.

\paragraph{Richer diagnosis.} The heuristic diagnoser's rule set could be
learned rather than hand-built (mining the incident history across runs
for recurring signature--remediation pairs), and the opt-in LLM
diagnoser could be grounded on retrieved evidence (traces, fleet state)
rather than the incident summary alone.

\paragraph{Agent-facing observability.} The case-study baseline, an
LLM agent diagnosing the fleet from the raw log in under a minute,
is less a curiosity than a signpost: the next consumer of
observability data may itself be an agent. The captured log already
has everything such a consumer needs (causal order, correlation ids,
host attribution, JSONL it can read with four shell commands); the
obvious extension is a first-class query surface, an MCP tool over
the log and its analyses, so that a supervising agent can run the
Doctor's gather-and-detect loop, or answer ad-hoc causal questions,
as part of the fleet it watches. The zero-marginal-cost principle of
R6 would then bound how much of that supervision runs on heuristics
versus paid model calls.

\medskip

The direction of the field is not in doubt: agent systems are growing in
both population and physical extent, and the questions operators ask are
increasingly the questions distributed systems have always asked, now
with a conversation attached. This thesis's contribution is to show that
the sixty-year-old toolbox of that discipline, from happened-before to
the shared log, is not merely applicable to the newest workload but is
its natural home.
